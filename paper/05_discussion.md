# Discussion

Three independent analytic approaches arrived at the same answer: in-person socialization is the strongest correlate of volunteering for the most socially disconnected generation in the available CPS-CEV record, and the initial step out of isolation yields the largest marginal civic return. The three methods—logistic regression, latent profile analysis, and gradient boosting—do not share assumptions about functional form, variable roles, or sample heterogeneity. Agreement under those conditions is not trivial.

Cacioppo and Cacioppo's (2014) theorized cycle of social withdrawal predicts exactly this pattern: even minimal social contact should disrupt the self-reinforcing dynamic of isolation and perceived social threat, producing disproportionate gains at the lowest end of the socialization continuum. The First Step Effect confirms that prediction across all five generations. Beyond this initial threshold, however, the generational trajectories diverged. Gen Z's volunteering probability plateaued at moderate socialization levels, a ceiling absent in every older cohort (see Figure 1). The plateau is inconsistent with classical social capital theory (Putnam, 2000), which predicts continuous returns from increased social contact for populations embedded in dense associational networks. For Gen Z, additional contact beyond a moderate threshold appears decoupled from the community-based institutions through which civic recruitment historically occurs.

Education was the only significant moderator of the socialization–volunteering–generation relationship (*p* = .009), amplifying the gradient more strongly for older cohorts—though the Gen Z plateau persisted even among degree holders, partially supporting H3a. Neither employment (*p* = .056) nor civic social media use altered the pattern, contrary to the predictions of H3b and H3c. The social media null (*p* = .969) warrants emphasis: the digital compensation effect posited by Bennett and Segerberg's (2012) connective action framework does not operate differently for digitally native young adults. Online civic activity offers no special substitute for face-to-face contact.

The pandemic explanation does not survive the data. The socialization × generation × post-COVID interaction was not significant (*p* = .420), and the pre-COVID First Step AME for Gen Z (10.7 points) was comparable to the full-sample estimate, supporting H3d. Gen Z's weaker conversion of social contact into volunteering was evident before COVID-19 and unchanged afterward. Pandemic recovery strategies alone will not resolve the civic engagement deficit among young adults—the deficit predates the pandemic. Twenge et al. (2019) documented that declines in adolescent socialization were well underway by 2017; the present analysis extends that work to its civic consequences.

The person-centered analysis reinforced these findings through a complementary lens. Gen Z was disproportionately concentrated in socially disconnected profiles (see Table 4): over half (50.6%) occupied the Isolated Disengaged or Politically Aware Isolated classes, and only 3.7% reached Fully Engaged status—consistent with H4 and H5. These proportions were essentially unchanged across the pre- and post-COVID periods, corroborating the temporal stability finding.

The Socially Active Non-Donors profile qualifies the socialization thesis. Members of this class reported the highest socialization scores (M = 4.70) of any profile yet volunteered at only 14.6%—below the sample mean. Socialization without institutional anchoring—organizational membership was near zero (M = 0.08) and charitable giving absent—did not generate the recruitment exposures that translate social contact into civic action. Robustness analysis confirmed this pathway: organizational membership attenuated the First Step Effect by approximately 30%, indicating that institutional embeddedness is a key mediating mechanism rather than socialization alone. Not all socializing is created equal. The distinction between bonding capital (close-knit relationships among similar individuals) and bridging capital (cross-cutting ties that connect individuals to new networks and resources) is central here (Putnam, 2000). The Socially Active Non-Donors appear to be high in bonding capital—frequent contact with friends and family—but low in the bridging capital that connects individuals to civic institutions. For Gen Z, whose socialization increasingly occurs in homogeneous peer groups rather than in the institutionally diverse settings (churches, civic associations, workplaces) that older cohorts navigated, this distinction may be particularly consequential.

A temporal shift in the profile structure is also informative. Gen Z's representation in the Activist Boycotter profile doubled from 5.9% pre-COVID to 10.7% post-COVID, even as the Isolated Disengaged proportion remained stable. The post-2020 period catalyzed a politicization pathway for a subset of Gen Z without altering the broader pattern of civic disengagement. The Activist Boycotter profile, which emphasizes political consumption over institutional volunteering (volunteering rate: 46.9%), may represent an emerging civic pathway that operates alongside—rather than as a substitute for—traditional voluntarism.

Socialization's dominance for Gen Z was confirmed by a model with no imposed interaction structure. In the gradient boosting analysis, socialization ranked as the single most important predictor of volunteering for Gen Z, outranking education—the top predictor for every older generation (see Figure 3). For older cohorts with established social networks, the traditional civic voluntarism resources of education and employment retain their predictive primacy (Verba et al., 1995). SHAP dependence plots independently recovered both the First Step Effect and the Gen Z plateau, confirming that these patterns are not artifacts of the parametric regression specification. The concave dose–response shape at the lowest socialization transition—not a statistically novel pattern in itself—carries civic implications that have not been previously documented across five generational cohorts.

These findings collectively challenge the prevailing narrative about generational differences in civic engagement. Dalton (2008) and others frame declining youth participation as a value shift from "duty-based" to "engaged" citizenship, in which younger cohorts express commitment through informal and digital channels. Our results point to a different explanation. Gen Z exhibited the highest predicted volunteering probability at zero socialization—27.3%, compared with 16.7% for similarly isolated Boomers (see Results). If declining youth volunteering reflected weakened civic motivation, one would expect lower baseline rates, not higher ones. Gen Z appears more civically motivated at the individual level but less able to convert that motivation into sustained participation. The plateau at moderate socialization levels is more consistent with an account in which Gen Z lacks the dense, multiplex social networks that Granovetter (1973) identified as conduits for weak-tie recruitment. In Putnam's (2000) formulation, they are not bowling alone by choice but rather scrolling together in a digital environment that sustains the perception of connection without generating the face-to-face encounters through which civic recruitment historically occurs.

The baseline paradox—Gen Z's higher volunteering probability at zero socialization—deserves further interpretation. One reading is that Gen Z possesses stronger intrinsic civic motivation, perhaps cultivated through increased exposure to social cause messaging in digital media and educational curricula that increasingly emphasize community service. An alternative reading is that the composition of completely isolated Gen Z respondents differs from that of completely isolated Boomers: an 18-year-old who reports no social contact may be a recently graduated high school student living with parents who volunteer, whereas a 65-year-old who reports no social contact may be a widowed retiree with genuinely no civic connections. The baseline comparison controls for demographics but cannot fully account for unobserved household-level civic socialization. Regardless of which interpretation holds, the more consequential finding is what happens above the baseline: Gen Z's volunteering probability plateaus where other generations' continues to climb, and it is this ceiling—not the floor—that explains the overall generational gap in volunteering rates.

The "scrolling together" metaphor captures a structural irony: Gen Z is the most *connected* generation in terms of digital communication, yet the most *disconnected* in terms of the face-to-face contact that drives civic recruitment. Social media platforms provide a continuous stream of information about social causes, volunteer opportunities, and community needs—but information alone does not substitute for the social obligation that arises from a face-to-face request. The null finding for civic social media use (*p* = .969) confirms this empirically: Gen Z members who actively use social media for civic purposes volunteer at the same rate as those who do not, once socialization frequency is controlled. Digital civic activity is not inert, but it does not function as a recruitment channel the way a personal encounter does. This disconnect between digital awareness and civic action may explain why Gen Z's volunteering probability plateaus at moderate socialization: those who socialize enough to encounter some recruitment opportunities do volunteer, but their social contexts—often peer-group gatherings without institutional anchoring—do not generate the escalating web of obligations and expectations that drove older cohorts toward progressively deeper civic engagement.

## Practical Implications

What should practitioners do differently? The First Step Effect points to a clear answer: target the initial transition out of social isolation. Robustness analyses confirmed that this threshold is genuine rather than an artifact of scale concavity: the categorical 0-to-1 transition exceeded smooth log-linear predictions by 2.4 to 4.7 percentage points across all generations, with the excess largest for Gen Z. The practical upshot is direct. Low-threshold, single-contact strategies should be preferred over sustained-commitment appeals. Creating one opportunity for in-person contact matters more than intensifying recruitment among already-connected individuals. Digital-only campaigns offer no special compensatory advantage (*p* = .969)—even for the cohort most dependent on digital communication. Volunteer organizations should invest in physical gathering spaces and initial-contact events, particularly on college campuses and in community settings where isolated young adults might be reached. Concretely, a campus volunteer center might host a monthly "coffee and causes" drop-in where students encounter service opportunities in a low-pressure social setting; a community organization could partner with local businesses to create "volunteer tasting" events where newcomers try a two-hour service project with no ongoing commitment; a library might embed volunteer sign-ups into existing social programming. The common thread is designing the social encounter first and the volunteer recruitment second—inverting the traditional model that assumes interested individuals will seek out opportunities on their own.

Community-based organizations occupy a strategic position in this landscape. Klinenberg's (2018) concept of "social infrastructure"—the physical spaces and institutions that facilitate social contact—provides a framework for understanding why investment in community gathering places, service-learning programs, and locally facilitated events may have civic returns that exceed their direct programmatic objectives. Cooperative Extension, embedded at the interface of campus and community, offers one institutional model: Extension educators could design programming that treats social connection not as a byproduct of volunteering but as a prerequisite for it. Other community-based institutions—libraries, recreation centers, faith communities, campus organizations—serve similar bridging functions and would benefit from the same reorientation.

For employers, the findings suggest that workplace-based volunteer programs may serve a dual function: they provide the structured social context that Gen Z respondents often lack outside the workplace, and they embed volunteer recruitment within the kind of organizationally anchored setting that the LPA analysis identified as critical. Companies that offer team-based volunteer days—where employees participate as a group rather than individually—may be more effective at engaging young workers precisely because they address the social barrier rather than relying solely on individual motivation.

At the policy level, the Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on loneliness and social isolation focused predominantly on health consequences. The present findings suggest extending that framing to encompass civic consequences: social disconnection does not merely increase the risk of heart disease and depression but also erodes the foundation on which democratic participation depends. Federal and state civic engagement initiatives—including AmeriCorps, national service programs, and campus-based volunteer centers—would benefit from incorporating a "social activation" component that addresses social isolation as a barrier to entry rather than assuming that motivation alone is sufficient to drive participation. The data also carry implications for how volunteering is measured and reported. Future measurement efforts should consider tracking not only whether individuals volunteer but the social pathway through which they were recruited—a distinction that the present findings suggest is essential for understanding generational differences in civic participation.

## Limitations

Several limitations temper the strength of these claims. The most consequential is the threat of reverse causation. The pooled cross-sectional design cannot establish whether socialization leads to volunteering or volunteering generates social contact—or whether both reflect a common disposition such as extraversion. People who volunteer meet others through their service, which increases their socialization frequency; the entire gradient could be partly driven by this pathway. However, two features of the results provide partial leverage against a purely reverse-causal account. First, the nonlinear threshold shape of the First Step Effect is more consistent with a recruitment mechanism—in which initial contact triggers awareness of opportunities—than with reverse causation, which would predict a more linear association. Second, the temporal stability of the pattern across four waves suggests a durable relationship rather than a context-dependent artifact. Future research using true panel data or quasi-experimental designs—potentially leveraging the CPS rotation group structure—could more definitively establish directionality.

Measurement constraints introduce additional caveats. The CESOCIALIZE item captures frequency of socialization but not its quality, depth, or content; "got together socially with friends, relatives, or neighbors" conflates kin-based socialization (primarily bonding capital), friendship networks, and neighbor contact (most directly tied to community recruitment). These domains have different theoretical relationships to volunteering, and the composite measure cannot disentangle them. The binary COVID coding (2017/2019 vs. 2021/2023) is approximate; the 2021 wave was administered during, not after, the pandemic. The PES16F variable measuring virtual versus in-person volunteering mode is available only for the 2023 wave, preventing systematic analysis of how volunteering modality interacts with social isolation.

Generational classification by birth cohort conflates age, period, and cohort effects. Supplementary analyses with nonlinear age controls (age-squared) confirmed that the socialization-by-generation interaction survived (*p* < .001), and the First Step AMEs were essentially unchanged, providing leverage against a purely age-based account—but a formal age-period-cohort decomposition would require additional data waves. All measures are self-reported and subject to social desirability bias, which may differentially affect reporting of socially valued behaviors such as volunteering.

## Future Directions

These limitations point toward a productive research agenda. First and most critically, the causal question demands longitudinal data. The CPS rotation group structure—in which households remain in the sample for four months, leave for eight, and return for four—offers a quasi-panel design that could establish whether changes in socialization within individuals precede changes in volunteering behavior. True panel studies with repeated measures of both constructs, such as the Panel Study of Income Dynamics or the Understanding America Study, could provide more definitive evidence of directionality.

Second, the CESOCIALIZE measure's breadth obscures important distinctions. Future research should disaggregate socialization by context (kin vs. friend vs. neighbor), modality (one-on-one vs. group), and institutional setting (organizationally embedded vs. informal). If the theoretical account offered here is correct—that institutionally anchored socialization drives civic recruitment while purely social contact does not—then context-specific measures should reveal differential associations with volunteering that the composite measure cannot capture.

Third, the null finding for civic social media use raises as many questions as it answers. The present study measured whether individuals *discuss civic topics* online, not whether they *socialize* online. A direct comparison of digital versus in-person socialization effects on volunteering—using measures of social media-based relationship maintenance, online community participation, and digital group membership—would test whether digital social contact can serve as a functional substitute for face-to-face recruitment.

Finally, the First Step Effect implies a specific intervention design: low-threshold, single-contact programs that target socially isolated young adults. Randomized controlled trials of such interventions—comparing, for example, a campus program that creates structured social encounters (the "social activation" approach) with a traditional volunteer recruitment campaign—would test whether the cross-sectional associations documented here translate into causal effects on volunteering behavior.

A fifth direction concerns cross-national comparison. The decline in youth socialization documented by Twenge et al. (2019) has been observed primarily in the United States and other Anglophone countries; whether similar patterns hold in societies with stronger institutional frameworks for youth civic participation—such as the Scandinavian countries with their tradition of popular movements and mandatory civil service options—would test the generalizability of these findings and potentially identify institutional models that buffer against the civic consequences of social isolation.

## Conclusion

The social disconnection of Generation Z is a civic engagement challenge as much as a health challenge. What the data show, with consistent convergence across methods and time, is that one step out of isolation is associated with a larger civic return than any subsequent increase in social frequency. If these associations reflect causal pathways, the path to volunteering begins not with appeals to duty or digital campaigns, but with creating the physical social encounters that make civic recruitment possible—the kind Klinenberg (2018) called social infrastructure. The present study measured civic social media use rather than social media-based socialization per se; future research should examine whether digital socialization serves as a functional substitute for face-to-face contact in generating civic recruitment. Addressing the decline in youth volunteering requires not only rethinking recruitment strategies but also rebuilding the institutional foundations through which civic participation has historically been sustained. The title of this paper invokes Putnam's metaphor deliberately. In 2000, Americans were bowling alone—participating in activities outside the organizational structures that once facilitated civic life. A quarter century later, the youngest Americans are not even bowling. They are scrolling together—maintaining the appearance of social connection through digital platforms that sustain awareness of community needs without generating the face-to-face encounters that translate awareness into action. The gap between knowing and doing, between digital empathy and physical participation, is where the civic consequences of the loneliness epidemic are most visible.
