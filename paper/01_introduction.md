# Introduction

In May 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General <red>formally identified</red> loneliness and social isolation as a public health epidemic, <red>documenting their significant associations with adverse mental health outcomes, physical well-being, and increased mortality risk</red> (Office of the Surgeon General, 2023). <red>Researchers have examined the health effects of social disconnection extensively, yet they have paid far less attention to its civic consequences, particularly its effects on volunteering.</red> That omission is consequential. A quarter century after Putnam (2000) documented the broad decline of associational life in *Bowling Alone*, emerging evidence suggests that the decline in social connection has continued, and accelerated among younger cohorts, with potential implications for the civic infrastructure that depends on volunteerism <blue>(Musick & Wilson, 2008; Verba et al., 1995)</blue>.

<blue>Volunteering in the United States has declined alongside declining social connection; evidence suggests recruitment pathways, rather than opportunity scarcity, may account for much of the change.</blue> Between 2005 and 2021, the <red>national</red> volunteering rate fell from 28.8% to 23.2%, the lowest <red>level</red> recorded <blue>since the Bureau of Labor Statistics began tracking the measure</blue> (AmeriCorps, 2022). <red>Participation among young adults declined even more sharply,</red> from 26.4% to 17.4%. <blue>Although organizations expanded their volunteer infrastructure, participation continued to decrease. These patterns are more consistent with weakened social recruitment mechanisms than with insufficient opportunities, though cross-sectional comparisons cannot confirm directionality.</blue>

Twenge et al. (2019) documented that in-person social interaction among U.S. adolescents declined substantially between 2003 and 2017, a trend that predates the COVID-19 pandemic and thus cannot be attributed solely to lockdown-era disruptions <blue>(Twenge, 2017)</blue>. If social interaction drives civic recruitment, as both social capital theory (Putnam, 2000) and the civic voluntarism model (Verba et al., 1995) suggest, then generational declines in face-to-face socialization may be associated with lower volunteering rates in ways that existing interventions fail to address. <blue>Social capital theory proposes that the association between socialization and volunteering operates through concrete, everyday interactions. Individuals encounter opportunities when acquaintances mention local needs at community events, coworkers invite them to participate in activities such as park cleanups, or neighbors encourage them to participate in fundraising efforts. These routine, low-threshold interactions historically facilitated civic engagement, and they rely on physical co-presence.</blue>

Understanding the civic consequences of social isolation is particularly <red>important</red> for the volunteering sector. Unlike voting or charitable giving, organized volunteering requires sustained <red>time investment</red> and coordination with others, making it especially dependent on social networks and in-person contact (Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2014; Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010). Social connections reduce entry barriers by providing practical information and the social accompaniment that lowers the threshold for initial participation. <red>However,</red> the empirical literature has treated social isolation primarily as a control variable rather than a central mechanism<red>. In addition,</red> existing research on generational differences tends to rely on simple cohort comparisons without examining how the *mechanisms* driving volunteering may differ across generations. Wilson (2012) identified social networks as a consistent predictor of volunteering but noted that the field lacks a clear account of how network effects vary across demographic subgroups. The question is whether declining youth volunteering is better explained by changing values or by the weakening of <red>social</red> connections that facilitate volunteering.

We address this gap using four waves of nationally representative CPS-CEV data (2017, 2019, 2021, 2023; *N* ≈ 201,000) to examine the association between in-person socialization frequency and volunteering across five generational cohorts. Two research questions guide <red>this study</red>, spanning variable-centered and person-centered approaches (see Purpose and Research Questions). By bracketing the COVID-19 pandemic, the four-wave design distinguishes stable cohort effects from pandemic-specific disruptions. The integrated approach, combining survey-weighted logistic regression and Latent Profile Analysis, extends the Surgeon General's health-focused framing to a civic engagement context. <red>In doing so, this study advances the literature by positioning social connection as a central mechanism in civic engagement, rather than a peripheral control, and by clarifying how generational differences shape pathways into volunteering.</red>
